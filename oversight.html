Inspector General Annual Report, FY 2025

The Office of the Inspector General (OIG) is LAUSD’s internal watchdog. It reports directly to the Board, audits contracts and programs, investigates fraud, and runs the District’s fraud hotline. Its annual report covers July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025.

What goes unaudited

Almost half of the OIG’s $10.6 million budget ($4.5 million) is bond money, which can only pay for work on bond-funded projects. The report says this limited the non-bond work it could do, so its audits focused heavily on bond-funded construction contracts. That means programs and contracts paid from the general fund get far less OIG scrutiny.

  • The OIG built a new data-analytics system covering four years and more than 100 million rows of District financial data, but it could only use it to review bond-funded spending.
  • Audits District management asked for but the OIG couldn’t do for lack of general funds: Bell Schedule Certification, Proposition 28 Arts Funding, Procurement of Common Goods at School Sites, and Student Body Accounts.
  • Audits the OIG’s own risk assessment called for but it couldn’t do: Attendance Management, Trend Analysis of Child Abuse Incidents, Special Education Compliance Oversight, and a follow-up on Maintenance & Operations tool purchases. Some audits were abandoned midway for lack of funding.
  • Staffing was cut: two audit manager and two senior investigator positions eliminated, five vacant senior auditor positions closed, and the head of investigations position sat vacant.

Key findings

Vendors owed LAUSD rebates and didn’t pay

Many LAUSD contracts require vendors to pay back a percentage of what the District spends (a “volume rebate”). In a sample of 31 of roughly 362 rebate contracts, 14 (45%) had unpaid or underpaid rebates totaling $615,772, about 30% of what was owed. Another $7 million in purchases tied to rebate contracts weren’t tagged in LAUSD’s SAP system, so rebates on them weren’t tracked at all. Because only 8.5% of contracts were sampled, the OIG says the real shortfall is likely “substantially higher.” The District has no formal process for collecting rebates. (Audit OA 25-1455)

IT purchasing: missing price checks

An audit of how LAUSD buys information technology services (OA 24-1452) reviewed three IT contracts and found the process generally followed District rules, but observed:

  • For one bidder, Vector Resources, Inc., the manufacturer’s price list and proof it was an authorized dealer were not available. Vector Resources is a partner on two contracts on this site: School Network Upgrades ($82.2M) and School PA & Network Upgrade ($3.5M). The audit does not say which three contracts it reviewed.
  • Procurement never sent the required cost-analysis request to the OIG for any of the three, and didn’t compare product prices to find the lowest-cost bidder for two of them (AAA Solutions and Responder Systems).
  • Procurement didn’t verify that LAUSD got the best discounts these bidders gave other schools or public agencies.
  • Vendor background (“due diligence”) reviews were inconsistent. LAUSD disagreed with the recommendation to create a due-diligence policy.

School credit cards

From July 2022 to March 2024, LAUSD employees made more than 227,000 credit-card (P-Card) purchases totaling over $78 million. The audit (OA 24-1457) found restricted items, including Apple iPads, iMacs and Nintendo gaming systems, bought without the required exception form; 31 potential split purchases to stay under approval limits; and more than half of cardholders and approvers hadn’t completed required training. At one school, items were shipped to a cardholder’s home and gift cards were bought without authorization. These are repeat findings from audits in 2017 and 2018. LAUSD disagreed with two of the 16 recommendations.

Tutoring vendor: background checks

An audit of HeyTutor (CA 24-1449) found no evidence of background checks and TB screenings for 13 of 25 tutors tested (52%), billing for a school that isn’t an LAUSD school, services billed before they were authorized, and extra training hours. It questioned $62,709.

Investigations

  • One investigation found record keeping and inventory control of certain District assets were “almost nonexistent,” leading to the theft of millions of dollars’ worth of materials over several years.
  • A principal hired a vendor owned by a teacher at the same school, then tried to pay the teacher through payroll after Procurement rejected the contract.
  • Several nepotism cases, including principals hiring their own adult children.
  • The OIG’s work led to more than $2.27 million in restitution and awards.

School security follow-up

A follow-up audit of 24 schools (OA 25-1456) found improvements, but 10 schools (42%) still had no surveillance cameras, 8 had no secure entry system, 3 couldn’t dial 911 directly from classrooms, and 3 had chained push-bar exit doors.

School bond audit, FY 2025

Voters have approved six LAUSD school construction bonds since 2002 (Measures K, R, Y, Q, RR and US), totaling $34.2 billion. State law (Prop 39) requires an independent audit every year. This one, by Simpson & Simpson CPAs, was issued April 24, 2026.

The financial statements themselves had no problems. But the performance audits, which check whether bond money was spent only on voter-approved projects, found:

  • IT contractor costs charged entirely to bonds. Information Technology Services contractors worked on both bond and non-bond projects, but their invoices were charged fully to bond funds and not corrected by year-end: $124,999 under Measure Q and $120,737 under Measure RR. LAUSD says it reallocated these in FY 2026. (The Measure RR amount is printed as “$120,737,50” in the report.)
  • An unresolved IT cost split. For a second year, LAUSD couldn’t support how it split the cost of an IT project (the “Soto re-configuration”) between bonds and the general fund. It gave auditors the same documents as the year before.
  • Non-construction items on bond projects: checkers and throwing darts ($1,212), and coffee, office supplies and a Ninja blender ($6,043).
  • Payroll based on budgets, not actual hours. Some employees’ pay was charged to bonds by a fixed budgeted percentage rather than time actually worked; FSD agreed it overcharged Measure Q $90,782 for three employees. The Personnel Commission finding is a repeat.
  • Software charged to bonds by estimates: Oracle, Autodesk, Bluebeam, DocuSign and other licenses were split between bond and non-bond use using old or budgeted figures. A job-order contracting license (Gordian) was overcharged to Measures RR and Y.
  • An Inspector General investigation charged to bonds couldn’t be verified as bond-eligible because the investigation is confidential.

The audit also notes that LAUSD entered about 195 construction contracts worth $720.7 million in 2024–25.
